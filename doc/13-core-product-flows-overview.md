# AutoSOl Core Product Flows

AutoSOl is a Solana payment orchestration product built around the idea that money movement should not be treated as a single generic transfer. Different business relationships require different levels of certainty, recipient review, sender control, and execution guarantees. In practice, that means the product offers three major payment models instead of one: normal recurring payments, commitment-based recurring payments, and payment requests. These flows are related, but they solve different coordination problems. Understanding the differences is important because the same payment amount and schedule can represent very different business intent depending on who initiates it, when funds are locked, and whether the recipient must explicitly accept terms before execution begins.

The normal recurring payment flow is the simplest model. In this flow, the sender creates a payment schedule directly, funds it, and the system executes the scheduled transfers when they become due. Conceptually, this is closest to an automated standing instruction. The sender already knows the recipient, already agrees with the payment terms, and does not need any additional pre-approval from the recipient before starting the stream of payments. The main value of this flow is convenience and predictability. Instead of manually sending the same transfer every week or month, the sender creates a schedule once and lets the executor process due payments over time. This model is appropriate when trust and terms are already settled outside the app and the sender simply wants reliable automation.

An example of a normal recurring payment is a freelancer paying a design retainer to a contractor every month, a founder sending a regular stipend to a contributor, or an individual sending recurring support to a family member. In each of these cases, the sender is the one deciding to initiate and fund the payment plan. The recipient does not need to negotiate through the product interface because the payment is being offered unilaterally by the payer. Operationally, the sender creates the schedule, chooses the token and dates, deposits the required funds into the schedule vault, and then each payment becomes executable when its scheduled time arrives. The executor wallet does not decide business logic; it only carries out due payments that the program already recognizes as valid and ready.

The key characteristic of the standard recurring payment model is that it is sender-driven and operationally direct. Once funded, the schedule behaves like a committed automation instruction with relatively low ceremony. The sender still has control over creating and, depending on status and policy, potentially pausing or cancelling in allowed scenarios, but the main user experience is optimized for speed and simplicity. This makes it a good fit for recurring vendor payments, internal treasury disbursements, regular allowances, contributor compensation, and scheduled token payouts where the payer does not need recipient-side acceptance workflow inside the product itself.

The commitment recurring payment flow adds a more formal agreement stage before the schedule becomes active. This is designed for situations where the recipient needs visibility into the exact terms before the money flow begins, and where the product should represent not just payment automation but an explicit bilateral commitment. A commitment starts as a proposal. The sender defines the payment terms, the schedule, the asset, and optionally the supporting note or contract content. The recipient can review those terms before accepting. After recipient acceptance, the commitment can be activated into a live schedule that the executor can process. This creates a stronger narrative and audit trail than a standard recurring payment because the recipient had an opportunity to review and approve the arrangement before the schedule became operational.

This flow is useful when the payment plan is part of an agreement rather than just a unilateral instruction. Examples include milestone-based creator retainers, grant disbursement commitments, payroll-like arrangements, scholarship or stipend commitments, and service contracts where the recipient wants to review the full note before accepting. A company could propose a six-month USDC commitment to a contractor, attach a markdown contract hosted through IPFS, and require the contractor to accept before activation. From the recipient’s perspective, the product acts as a transparent review surface, showing payment count, timing, token, memo, and contract note. From the sender’s perspective, the commitment flow creates a clearer record that the counterparty saw and accepted the terms.

The commitment model matters because it changes the trust boundary. In a normal recurring payment, the sender assumes the recipient will simply receive funds when they arrive. In a commitment, the sender is effectively saying that the payment arrangement itself is something both parties should acknowledge. This is especially useful when the payment schedule has reputational, legal, or operational significance beyond the transfer itself. The note or contract component is therefore not cosmetic. It allows AutoSOl to represent the payment schedule as part of a broader structured obligation. That makes the feature relevant for professional services, treasury operations, sponsorship agreements, subscription commitments with negotiated terms, and any arrangement where a record of acceptance is useful.

The payment request flow reverses the direction of initiation. Instead of the payer proposing a schedule to a recipient, the payee creates a request that asks the payer to approve and fund the schedule. This is fundamentally different from both standard recurring payments and commitments because the economic initiative comes from the party expecting to be paid. The requester defines the amount, token, schedule, and supporting note, and then the payer decides whether to accept, decline, or ignore the request. If accepted, the schedule is created and funded by the payer, and only then does the automation begin. In other words, payment requests turn the product into a structured “request-to-pay” system for one-time or recurring obligations.

This flow is especially useful in situations where the service provider, organization, or merchant knows what should be paid but cannot directly initiate the customer’s funds. Examples include a SaaS company requesting monthly subscription payments, a freelancer sending a recurring billing request for a support retainer, a DAO contributor requesting a stipend plan from treasury, or a landlord requesting monthly rent via a predefined schedule. It is also useful in B2B payment operations where accounts receivable teams want to propose structured payment plans instead of sending informal invoices that still require manual settlement every cycle.

The product value of payment requests comes from combining invoicing intent with on-chain execution structure. Traditional invoices are static documents that still leave collection and repeated follow-up to humans. A payment request in AutoSOl is more powerful because, once the payer accepts, the system can immediately move from proposal state to an executable recurring payment schedule. That reduces operational friction while keeping control with the payer until acceptance. It also provides transparency to both sides: the requester can track whether the payer has approved, declined, or whether the request remains pending, and the payer can review exact schedule terms before funding anything.

Although these three flows are distinct, they are designed to work as a coherent product family. All three revolve around scheduled on-chain payments, executor-based due payment processing, token-aware accounting, and a consistent status model. What changes from one flow to another is not the executor’s job, but the agreement model that precedes execution. Standard recurring payments are best understood as direct automation. Commitments are best understood as bilateral scheduled agreements that the recipient explicitly accepts before activation. Payment requests are best understood as payee-initiated proposals that the payer may approve and fund. The shared infrastructure gives the product consistency, while the different initiation and acceptance models make it suitable for a much broader set of real financial relationships.

From a product strategy perspective, this distinction is important because it moves AutoSOl beyond the category of “another recurring transfer app.” Standard recurring payments cover the basic automation use case. Commitments introduce auditability, transparency, and recipient consent for higher-trust or contract-like flows. Payment requests introduce receivables and subscription-style initiation from the payee side. Together, they let the product serve individuals, freelancers, organizations, treasury teams, and service businesses using a single payment execution engine but different business workflows. That layered design is one of the strongest conceptual advantages of the project.

A useful way to think about the three flows is through a simple decision lens. If the payer already knows they want to send funds on a schedule and just needs automation, the standard recurring payment flow is the right choice. If the payer wants to formalize the schedule as a proposal that the recipient should review and accept before activation, the commitment flow is the correct model. If the payee wants to ask the payer to approve and fund a schedule, the payment request flow is the best fit. All three eventually converge on scheduled payment execution, but they start from different business realities. That is exactly why the product needs all three.

In practical usage, these features can coexist inside one organization or one user’s workflow. A treasury team might use normal recurring payments for internal disbursements, commitments for contractor or grant obligations that need explicit recipient review, and payment requests when external vendors or service partners need to propose a payment plan back to treasury. A creator economy platform might use recurring payments for platform-driven payouts, commitments for campaign or sponsorship agreements, and payment requests for talent submitting recurring invoices. A SaaS business could use commitments for negotiated enterprise retainers and payment requests for customer subscription approvals. The flows are not competing alternatives; they are complementary tools for different stages and directions of economic coordination.

At the execution layer, the app still depends on the same fundamental operational model: schedules become due over time, authorized executor wallets submit execution transactions, and the program enforces what is valid. This means the major product complexity is not just technical execution, but representing intent correctly before execution starts. That is why acceptance status, proposal state, request state, activated schedule references, and clear UI terminology matter so much in this project. The business meaning of a payment plan must remain understandable from proposal through execution, otherwise the automation loses trust value.

In summary, AutoSOl should be understood as a scheduled payment system with three primary agreement models. Standard recurring payments solve direct payer-driven automation. Commitment recurring payments solve structured bilateral acceptance before activation. Payment requests solve payee-driven initiation where the payer remains in control until approval. Each flow maps to a distinct real-world use case, and together they form the product’s core functional identity.
